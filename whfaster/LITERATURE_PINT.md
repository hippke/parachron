# Parareal must move into mean elements to win

The literature does not contain a single measured multicore wall-clock speedup for one long planetary trajectory. Everything that exists is a projection or an iteration count: Saha-Stadel-Tremaine's 50x on 1000 processors, Dai et al.'s ~66x on 1000 processors, and Gander-Hairer's proofs that the convergent window of parareal shrinks to the square root of the coarse accuracy for near-integrable systems. Read alongside the project's own measurements, the failure of WH-coarse parareal is a **Jacobian mismatch in the action (semi-major-axis) directions, amplified by Kepler shear**, and not a phase offset. A constant frequency error in the coarse solver cancels exactly in the parareal update, so "calibrating" a coarse WH map cannot by itself create contraction. Only one family of ideas attacks the actual mechanism while dropping the coarse-to-fine cost ratio r from ≥0.4 to ~0.01: **parareal (or chunk quasi-Newton) done in mean elements**, where a first-order near-identity transform removes the short-period terms that couple to shear, a secular-plus-Kepler map serves as the coarse step, and the fixed point is still the exact fine trajectory. On back-of-envelope models (inference, untested) this gives about **2.4-4.6x on 10 cores and 1.2-2.4x on 5 cores**, rising to 1.5-3x on 5 cores with the project's two-systems-per-call pairing. That clears the "2x with 5-10x cores" bar only if the iteration count stays at k ≤ 3. Hardware latency is irrelevant at slice granularity: one 384-byte handoff per 6 ms slice costs 0.02%. At step granularity it is fatal: a 20 ns same-CCD link already turns a 100 ns step into 140 ns. The decisive first experiment is cheap, takes minutes of compute, and can be checked against the convergence the project has already measured. It is a direct measurement of the 1 kyr slice Jacobian, compared with each candidate coarse model's Jacobian.

*Evidence labels used throughout: "measured" means numbers from experiments in the cited paper or the project log; "published estimate" means projections or theoretical speedups stated by the authors; "inference" means the research team's notes or this report's own back-of-envelope derivations, none of which have been tested.*

## No paper has measured single-trajectory multicore speedup, and theory says why

The closest astrophysical precedents are all estimates. Saha, Stadel & Tremaine assign each processor a time interval, not a planet. They solve all implicit-midpoint steps of a window as one nonlinear system by a derivative-free Newton/waveform-relaxation iteration that contracts by about ε ≈ 1e-3 (the planet/Sun mass ratio) per sweep. Their **~50x on 1000 processors at ~1″/century was projected from serial runs, not measured**, and in the worst case the iteration count grows in proportion to the number of steps ([Saha, Stadel & Tremaine](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/astro-ph/9605016)). The only follow-up located, Fukushima's 1999 review, repeats the projection ([Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/international-astronomical-union-colloquium/article/parallelvector-integration-methods-for-dynamical-astronomy/54B198E8FA1431488C8683F21C295455)). Jiménez-Pérez & Laskar keep the perturbation-derivative term SST97 drops and frame the scheme as a special case of parareal. They also state that its corrector is expensive and that earlier parareal schemes needed more than 10 iterations to reach the near-machine-precision local errors long-term integrations require ([Jiménez-Pérez & Laskar](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1106.3694)). Dai, Le Bris, Legoll & Maday ran the outer Solar System (Sun + 5 bodies) with a Kepler-only coarse solver. Energy met tolerance at k ≥ 8, and the trajectory matched the fine solver at **k = 15 iterations (measured), a theoretical speedup of ~66 on 1000 processors (published estimate)**. They also showed that symmetrizing parareal alone does not stop energy drift, while symmetric projection onto the energy manifold does ([Dai et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1011.6222)). At 5-10 cores, k = 15 means a slowdown.

Gander & Hairer supply the theory that explains the project's measurements. For coarse accuracy ε_G, the convergent parareal window is O(ε_G⁻¹) for the harmonic oscillator, **O(ε_G^-1/2) for integrable and near-integrable systems**, and O(1) for chaotic ones (set by e^{Lt}). On a Kepler test, a coarse solver accurate to 1e-7 was insufficient, while an order-8 coarse solver at 1e-14 needed 1-2 iterations over windows above 20,000. They give the pipelined cost N·c_G + K(c_G + c_F) and put typical speedups at only 2-4x ([Gander & Hairer](https://www.unige.ch/~hairer/preprints/parareal-gh.pdf)). The Solar System's ~5 Myr Lyapunov time is far longer than the project's 1-10 kyr slices, so chaos is not what failed. The near-integrable shear limit is ([inference, research notes](https://www.unige.ch/~hairer/preprints/parareal-gh.pdf)).

Outside astrophysics, the strongest relevant results replace the coarse model rather than tune it, and none has a wall-clock number on a multi-frequency Hamiltonian problem. Ariel, Kim & Tsai's phase-aligned multiscale parareal converged in **1 iteration for all ε from 0.2 to 0.001 (measured iteration counts), where conventional coarse integrators needed up to 12,200 iterations or hit the cap**. Alignment failed near a passage through resonance, and Hamiltonian variants were declared out of scope ([Ariel, Kim & Tsai](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1503.02094v1)). Haut & Wingate's asymptotic PinT averages the nonlinear term along the exact fast flow. They estimate ~100x at ε = 1e-2, more than 10x standard parareal (published estimate), and note validity only over O(1) times ([Haut & Wingate](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1303.6615)). Ruprecht traces parareal instability for wave problems to coarse phase (dispersion) error ([Ruprecht](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1701.01359)). Krzysik et al. fix MGRIT for advection by modifying the coarse operator so that its truncation error matches the fine one, and report "a handful" of iterations ([Krzysik et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.04936v2)). Learned-correction parareal reports up to 125x on dissipative PDEs ([RandNet-Parareal](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.06225v1)), but nothing on phase-dominated Hamiltonian systems.

The general-purpose sequence-parallel methods fail for a symplectic map. A symplectic map has Jacobian determinant 1 and a symmetric Lyapunov spectrum, so the contracting regime where DEER-type Newton methods reach O((log T)²) never applies. Gonzalez et al. measured **12,700-30,000 DEER steps for chaotic systems with T = 30,000 steps, about 10x slower than serial on an H100** ([Gonzalez et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.16817v4)). On a trained GRU, every parallel variant was slower than sequential: 221 ms against 96 ms ([Gonzalez et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.19115v3)). For a symplectic HMC leapfrog, quasi-DEER "often needed close to L iterations" ([Zoltowski et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.18413v2)). For chaotic systems, iteration counts grow in proportion to the time domain, so cost grows like T² ([Wang et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1211.2437)). The 2026 Lyapunov-weighted criterion cuts parareal on Lorenz from **K = 112-113 to K = 2 at N = 128 (algorithmic S ≈ 28, no wall-clock)**, but only by converging in a statistical sense, not pointwise ([Antonucci et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.00855v1)). Formally, iterating a general map is P-complete, so any escape must exploit structure ([P-completeness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-complete)). For this problem the one usable structure is near-integrability with ε ≈ 1e-3. MCPI and PFASST do not transfer either. MCPI's parallel gains come from batches of trajectories, with segments of at most about one orbit ([DTIC ADA626629](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA626629.pdf)). PFASST on 2 time-processes was slightly slower than serial SDC at 1e-13 accuracy even with expensive O(N²) forces ([Speck et al.](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/90540/1/speckr_contrib.pdf)).

## Twenty-nanosecond links cannot rescue a 100-nanosecond step

The project assumed 50-100 ns per cache-line transfer. That is right for server meshes and pessimistic for a desktop CCD. **Zen 5 (Ryzen 9 9950X) measures ~18.6-20.5 ns between cores in one CCX, with no SMT-sibling advantage, and ~180 ns across CCDs** ([Anandtech](https://at-web1.www.anandtech.com/show/21524/the-amd-ryzen-9-9950x-and-ryzen-9-9900x-review/3)). Chips and Cheese measured "nearly 200 ns" cross-cluster ([Chips and Cheese](https://old.chipsandcheese.com/2024/08/14/amds-ryzen-9950x-zen-5-on-desktop/)), and AGESA 1.2.0.2 cut the cross-CCD figure by ~2.4x ([HotHardware](https://hothardware.com/news/zen-5-c2c-latency-bug-fixed)). Sapphire Rapids averages **59 ns one-way intra-socket (81 ns worst) and 138 ns cross-socket** ([Chips and Cheese](https://chipsandcheese.com/p/core-to-core-latency-data-on-large-systems)). Other one-way medians: Zen 3 at 16-17 ns intra-CCX and 84-85 ns cross, Alder Lake P-cores at ~35 ns, Ice Lake SP at ~51 ns ([nviennot](https://github.com/nviennot/core-to-core-latency)). Intel user interrupts cost 1360 cycles end-to-end ([xUI summary](https://danglingpointers.substack.com/p/extended-userspace-interrupts)), so the only viable handoff is spin-polling a padded cache line, HFT-style.

These numbers still do not rescue intra-step parallelism, because WHFast512 has already spent the parallelism. The eight planets sit in SIMD lanes. The step's ~100 ns is the length of a dependency chain (Kepler Newton iterations, then the force reduction), not a lane count, and splitting lanes across cores shortens no chain. The research notes' models (inference) are as follows. Two cores each drifting four planets, with one exchange per step, give 100 + 2×20 = 140 ns on Zen 5 (**0.71x**) and 200 + 120 = 320 ns on SPR (**0.63x**). Overlapping step n's Kepler drift with a kick computed from predicted positions is bounded by Amdahl at 1/0.64 = 1.56x even with free correction. Realistically it reaches **0.93-1.08x**, and an uncorrected predicted kick breaks time symmetry and creates secular energy drift. Delayed coupling or two-subsystem waveform relaxation needs ~log(1e-16)/log(1e-3) ≈ 5-6 sweeps per window for a 2x split. Bitwise value speculation is impossible for a chaotic floating-point state. The project's own measurement that the interaction step is throughput-bound, with tree reductions slower, closes the remaining intra-step angle (project `RESEARCH_LOG.md`).

The same facts make time slicing essentially free of communication. A 1 kyr slice is ~61,000 steps, or ~6 ms on Zen 5. One 384-byte state handoff, even through a 1 µs OpenMP fork/join, costs **~0.02% of a slice**, so the CCD boundary, mesh hops and socket do not matter (inference). The binding quantities are purely algorithmic: iterations k and cost ratio r. The hardware does offer one free multiplier. Parallel-in-time cores integrate independent slices, so each core can advance two slices per kernel call with the project's `steps_pair`, measured at **1.22-1.3x per system on AVX-512** (project `RESEARCH_PLAN.md`). This turns P cores into ~1.25P slice-equivalents (inference).

## The measured failure is an action-row Jacobian mismatch amplified by shear

Two corrections to the project's framing change which leads are worth pursuing.

First, the project's speedup model S = N/(k + (k+1)N r) is the non-pipelined cost. Gander-Hairer's pipelined variant gives S_p = N/(N r + k(1+r)). Applied to the measured configurations, it lifts the 0.3-0.5 range to **0.77 (dt_G = 8 d, k = 3), 0.85 (12 d, k = 4) and ~1.0 (16 d, k = 4) on 10 slices**. Pipelining therefore narrows the gap but leaves WH-coarse parareal at break-even (inference from the project's measured k and r). For 2x on 10 cores the pipelined model needs r ≤ 0.25 at k = 2 or r ≤ 0.15 at k = 3. On 5 cores it needs r ≤ 0.07 at k = 2.

Second, parareal's update uses only the difference G(U^{k+1}) − G(U^k). **Any state-independent coarse error, including a constant mean-motion offset, cancels exactly.** The error recursion is driven by (F′ − G′), the mismatch of slice Jacobians, and propagated by G′, which contains Kepler shear ∂λ/∂a = (3/2)nΔT/a. For Mercury over 1 kyr, (3/2)n₁ΔT ≈ 3.9e4. Across a 10-slice window, an error of δ in a mean semi-major axis grows to ~4e5·δ in mean longitude. This report therefore infers a concrete design criterion. **The action rows of (F′ − G′) must be below ~1e-7 to 1e-8, while the angle rows can tolerate ~1e-2.** This is where WH-coarse parareal breaks down. A coarse WH at 16-24 d gets Mercury's short-period and step-size-aliasing terms wrong, and the project measured these as non-monotonic, phase-dependent λ₁ errors with an envelope ~dt¹⁰. Those terms are state-dependent, so their derivatives enter the action rows at well above 1e-7. The project's own note that a secular model "lacks the short-period terms that dominate the Jacobian mismatch in λ" points at the same thing (project `RESEARCH_LOG.md`). Calibration, alignment or learned corrections of a WH coarse solver help only through the initial-guess error e₀. That saves about one iteration via k ≈ log(e₀/tol)/log(1/ρ), and it does not change the contraction ρ.

The same analysis fixes the stopping rule, and the rule has to be stricter in actions than the project's position tolerance. The parareal fixed point is the fine trajectory whatever G is, so a biased coarse model, even a degree-4 secular model that is "orders of magnitude" too stable ([Mogavero, Hoang & Laskar](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2305.01683)), cannot bias the converged answer. Only the residual boundary jumps can. Over 5 Gyr at 1 kyr per slice there are 5e6 boundaries. To keep the rms energy error near the project's Brouwer law (1e-16·√N, i.e. ~5.5e-11 at 3e11 steps), per-boundary energy jumps need **rms ≲ 1e-14 and mean ≲ 1e-18** (inference). The project's measured F noise of 1.3e-9 AU per kyr is almost entirely along-track phase. The coherent translation of "statistical acceptance" for this problem is therefore a round-off-equivalent rule (inference). Each boundary jump must look like the round-off the fine solver itself injects over one slice: ~2.5e-14 in actions and the measured F noise in angles. Such a trajectory is, by construction, one more member of the serial ensemble for energy, secular frequencies and instability statistics.

### Mean-element parareal is the only lead aimed at the mechanism

This lead proposes the following steps (inference). Restrict each boundary state to mean Jacobi/Poincaré variables using a first-order-in-mass near-identity (Lie) transform. Advance the mean actions as constant, the mean longitudes at Kepler mean motion plus the O(μ) secular mean-motion correction, and the eccentricity and inclination vectors with a secular map (even Laplace-Lagrange-level should do over 1 kyr, where g₁ moves Mercury's perihelion only ~0.03 rad). Then lift back. The transform removes Mercury's short-period semi-major-axis terms, which are of order the Venus mass ratio (~1e-5) in the osculating action rows, down to O(μ²). That is the scale the shear criterion needs. G no longer has to resolve Mercury, so r falls to ~0.01 even if the transform is evaluated numerically, for example with Haut-Wingate kernel averaging of the interaction along Kepler orbits ([Haut & Wingate](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1303.6615)). That costs ~1000 kick evaluations per boundary, against ~61,000 steps per slice. The design is the micro-macro parareal of Legoll, Lelièvre & Samaey, whose convergence is limited by the macro model's error ([Legoll et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1204.5926)). It is also the slow-variable route both research threads flagged as the only place where r ≪ 0.05 is algebraically possible. Mogavero & Laskar show that a first-order averaged inner-Solar-System model with leading GR is accurate enough to reproduce the Lyapunov exponent and Mercury's 5 Gyr high-eccentricity statistics ([Mogavero & Laskar 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.14976)), which supports the transform's fidelity. Their degree-4 truncation's instability deficit does not matter here, because G only steers convergence. Calibrating the mean motions and the great-inequality term from previously converged slices reduces e₀, which is where the "learned F − G correction" idea earns its place. With ρ ≈ 1e-3 to 1e-2 and a calibrated e₀, k = 2-4 follows. The main risk is that near-resonant terms not captured at first order (the Jupiter-Saturn 5:2 great inequality, Uranus-Neptune near-2:1) or O(μ²) mean-motion terms leave ρ ≥ 0.1 for Jupiter and Saturn. Their mass ratio is ~1e-3 and their shear over 1 kyr is ~800, so the giants rather than Mercury may set k. A second risk is expansion accuracy at Mercury's e ≈ 0.2, which numerical averaging sidesteps. **Cheapest falsifier:** the Jacobian-mismatch test below. If the action-row mismatch of R∘F∘L against G exceeds ~1e-7 for any planet over 1 kyr slices, after the great-inequality term is included, drop the lead.

### Chunk quasi-Newton is the fallback if analytic transforms are too hard

This lead replaces G entirely with an approximate slice Jacobian, U_j ← F(U_{j−1}^k) + J_j·(U_{j−1}^{k+1} − U_{j−1}^k). The sequential path then costs only 48×48 matrix-vector products (r ≈ 0). Pure Kepler shear is exact for the angle-action coupling but leaves the osculating action rows wrong at ~1e-5, so it should fail the criterion (inference). The repair is to compute the eight action rows explicitly. Because the map is symplectic, J^T = ΩJ⁻¹Ω⁻¹, so the rows ∂Λ_end/∂U₀ come from eight tangent vectors integrated backward along the reversible slice rather than 48 forward ones (inference). They must then be reused across M slices with Broyden/Krylov updates, the Krylov-enhanced-parareal idea of reusing the span of earlier fine results (Gander-Petcu lineage; no numbers retrieved, so this is inference). The cost is c_x = 8c_v/M, where c_v is the unmeasured tangent cost of 1-2 steps. With M = 10 this gives ~1.2, which halves the speedup; with M = 100 it gives 0.12. The main risk is that slice Jacobians depend on synodic phases and decorrelate slice to slice, so reuse fails. Exact chunk Newton is out regardless: about 75 step-equivalents per step, i.e. S ≈ 0.04 (research notes' estimate). The quadratic basin is also tiny, because shear squares into the second-order residual. **Cheapest falsifier:** in the same Jacobian test, measure ρ for J_F reused from m = 1, 10, 100 slices earlier. If ρ > 0.3 at m = 10, drop the lead.

### Energy projection and round-off-equivalent acceptance are mandatory add-ons, not engines

Dai et al.'s symmetric projection onto the energy manifold, optionally extended to angular momentum, takes at most 2 Newton steps on a 48-dimensional state per boundary and costs nothing measurable ([Dai et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1011.6222)). Projecting each boundary state onto the energy of F(U_{j−1}) enforces the ≲1e-14 energy-jump budget by construction. Iterations can then stop at the phase tolerance instead of the stricter action tolerance, saving roughly one iteration (×1.0-1.5 on the host lead; inference). Their own 15-iteration result shows that projection creates no contraction. The risk is a systematic projection bias at the 1e-18-per-boundary level the budget demands. **Falsifier:** add symmetric projection to the existing `pr.py` at dt_G = 12 d. Check that iterations to the action tolerance drop, and that the mean boundary energy jump over 16 runs is consistent with zero.

### Calibrating a coarse WH map cannot remove the step-size-resonance mismatch

Frequency-fitted mean motions, Ariel-Kim-Tsai-style per-planet alignment and Krzysik-style "modified coarse operators" all improve e₀, but the measured failure is in (F′ − G′). Angle-wrapped element corrections already perform most of the alignment, and with eight incommensurate frequencies no single time shift aligns all phases ([Ariel, Kim & Tsai](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1503.02094)). The cost ratio has a floor at the measured r = 0.26 for 24 d, and that configuration showed no contraction. The best case on 10 cores, k = 2 at 24 d, gives 1.95x. The 16 d configuration with k = 3 gives 1.18x. One confound is worth ruling out: at 24 d Mercury sits at dt/P ≈ 0.27, inside the regime the project found where the 19-term Stiefel series is not converged. That is probably too small an effect to matter at 0.1 rad errors (inference). **Falsifier:** re-run `pr.py` at 16 and 24 d with fitted per-planet δn and a local linear F − G model learned from previous slices. If k at 16 d does not fall to ≤2 and 24 d still fails to contract, stop.

### Lyapunov-weighted acceptance buys nothing at kyr slices

At 1 kyr the chaos factor is e^{1 kyr / 5 Myr} ≈ 1.0002, so Antonucci-style weighting collapses to the pointwise rule ([Antonucci et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.00855v1)). Weighting by the slice's shear-dominated Lipschitz constant would instead license unphysical action jumps. The weighting only matters with Myr slices and a secular G, where k ≈ 2 would give ~4.5x on 10 cores. The product would then be a stitched, statistically equivalent trajectory, and the "unchanged Mercury instability statistics" goal is at risk. The instability rate is non-stationary: its log grows linearly from 1.3 to 5 Gyr ([Abbot et al. 2023](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2212.14844)). Gorynina et al. do show that statistical accuracy without trajectory accuracy is achievable in Langevin MD ([Gorynina et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.10508v1)). **Falsifier:** at 5 Myr slices with k = 2, check whether energy and AMD discontinuities stay ≲1e-14. If they do not, the stitched path is not a valid ensemble member.

### Waveform relaxation and rare-event methods solve different problems

A WH-structured SST97 variant could Picard-iterate the kicks over a window, with Kepler shear handled by an exact linear prefix recurrence. It still needs 5-6 full-cost sweeps for machine precision on top of SST's admitted arithmetic overhead, so S ≤ P/6 ≈ 1.7 on 10 cores before overheads (inference from [SST97](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/astro-ph/9605016)). **Falsifier:** a numpy prototype on a 1000-step window that counts sweeps to 1e-15; kill if the count exceeds P/2. For the statistics goal, rare-event splitting is the right tool. QDMC estimated the ~1e-4 probability of Mercury instability within 2 Gyr at **up to 100x lower cost than direct simulation (measured)**, using 1008 trajectories ([Abbot et al. 2021](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2106.09091)). Plain WH at 3.16 d reproduces the statistics ([Abbot et al. 2023](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2212.14844)). Neither shortens one trajectory. ParSplice-style splicing needs metastable Markov states and stationary segment starts ([ParSplice](https://efthimioskaxiras.scholars.harvard.edu/publications/long-time-dynamics-through-parallel-trajectory-splicing)). The measured non-stationarity of the instability rate already falsifies that.

## Recommended experimental program, in order

**Step 1: the slice-Jacobian test (one core, minutes of compute).** Take 20 epochs along a 1 Myr serial run. Compute the true 1 kyr slice Jacobian J_F in canonical Jacobi/Poincaré elements by 48 finite-difference or variational runs (~6e7 steps in total, about 10 s of kernel time). Compare it, separately for the action and angle rows, with:

- WH-coarse at 8, 12, 16 and 24 d;
- pure Kepler shear;
- the mean-element-conjugated secular map;
- J_F reused from m = 1, 10 and 100 slices earlier.

The WH-coarse comparison is the validation step: the predicted ρ must reproduce the measured outcome (contraction at 8-16 d, none at 24 d) before the predictor is trusted. Continue only with leads whose predicted pipelined ρ_eff is below ~0.05.

**Step 2: restriction quality.** Implement the first-order near-identity transform, analytically or by kernel averaging, with the great-inequality term. Apply it to a 10 kyr fine run and require residual short-period content in mean semi-major axes below ~1e-8 for every planet.

**Step 3: mean-element parareal in the existing `pr.py`.** Run 10 slices of 1 kyr, then 10 kyr. Measure k to the round-off-equivalent tolerance (actions ~2.5e-14 per kyr; angles at the measured F noise) with calibrated e₀. Stop if k > 4.

**Step 4: add symmetric energy and angular-momentum projection.** Re-measure k and the mean and rms of boundary jumps. Run the step-3 calibration tests on lead 4 in parallel as a cheap control.

**Step 5: build a C driver.** Use persistent pinned spin-waiting threads, a pipelined schedule and `steps_pair` pairing, and run on 5 and 10 cores. Report wall time and core-hours over ≥10 Myr, plus the 5 Gyr projection.

**Step 6: validate against the accuracy contract.** Run the 16×1e9 bias ensemble with boundary-jump statistics, compare secular frequencies over 20-50 Myr against serial runs, then test instability statistics on a cluster. Run that last test as an ensemble or QDMC, which remains the efficient route for the statistics goal regardless of step 5.

If step 1 rejects both mean-element and quasi-Newton variants, the conclusion is firm. Single-trajectory multicore speedup is not reachable for this integrator, and cores should go to ensembles and pairing.

## Conclusion

The reframing matters more than any single lead. Parareal for WH does not fail because the coarse solver's phases drift; constant drifts cancel. It fails because small, state-dependent errors in the actions get multiplied by Kepler shear (~4e5 for Mercury across a 10 kyr window), and a coarse WH map cannot avoid that without resolving Mercury, which pins r ≥ 0.4. The fix therefore has to remove short-period terms from the action rows, not refine the coarse integrator. That points to mean elements, a near-identity transform and a secular map whose cost is almost zero. The fixed-point property means its modelling errors cost iterations, not accuracy. A second consequence is that the strict constraint is energy-jump bookkeeping at 5e6 boundaries (rms ≲1e-14, mean ≲1e-18), not trajectory tolerance. This makes symmetric energy projection a requirement rather than an option. Nothing in the literature has done this for an N-body problem, so success would be publishable and failure is cheap to detect: the Jacobian test either shows action-row mismatches below ~1e-7 within a day of work, or it closes the parallel-in-time route for good.
